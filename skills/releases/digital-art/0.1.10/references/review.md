# Four-part creative rubric

Use the same criteria to compare written directions and to improve the selected work. In a treatment, score the evidence described in words. In a preview, score what is visible, audible or felt in use at the intended viewing conditions.

## Precise expression

| Criterion | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Intent | Name what a viewer should notice, feel or reconsider. Start from what the creator has already decided. For a sparse brief, propose associations and a direction the creator can judge. |
| Image and association | Identify a familiar shape, material, action, cultural association or perceptible rule that gives the audience a way in. Say what the intended audience can infer from it. An abstract piece can establish a rule through density, resistance, repetition or another perceptible relationship. |
| Expressive mechanism | Point to the action or relationship that carries the idea. A screen pattern that becomes something a figure can pass through does several jobs within one event. When two unlikely sources are fused, identify the behavior they share and what each changes about the other. Describe what the artwork would lose if the material, image or action were replaced. |
| Development | Name what changes in what the audience sees or understands. Look for a cause, a relationship between states, accumulating pressure, or a deliberate hold. In a still, read the progression through focus, scale and layers. Use exact times or values when they matter to the expression. A threshold, relative amount or response curve can guide the work when the experience determines the timing or magnitude. |
| Sensory coherence | Explain how the applicable choices in light, color, surface, motion, sound, text and interaction carry the idea together. If sound is present, identify a moment whose meaning or feeling it changes. If participation matters, identify what the gesture makes possible. |

## Visual appeal

| Criterion | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Composition and first glance | Describe one complete view, including its largest shapes, negative space, focal point and what the eye follows. At intended scale, check what is visible at a glance and what rewards a closer look. |
| Color and material | Name relationships between the colors and identify a property of the surface that is legible in light or motion. Assess the image in silence when it is also scored for sound. |
| Visual invention and recall | Point to the image, change in scale, juxtaposition or transformation worth pausing on. Identify the particular choice a viewer could remember after seeing the piece. |

## Emotional resonance

| Criterion | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Emotional entry | Identify a concrete moment or sensory detail that lets the viewer bring their own experience. Hair over a drain and water pooling around a foot offer a tangible reason for unease. |
| Emotional development | Name what the audience first feels, what they come to expect and which perceivable cue intensifies or changes it. A surprising turn should be unexpected before it happens and make sense once seen. For a still, compare the first impression with what becomes apparent on a longer look. Let a deliberate hold gain meaning through an observable detail. |

## Production feasibility

| Criterion | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Feasibility of the defining effect | Name the hardest effect essential to the experience, including which paths, deformations, contacts or performances have to stay legible. Identify the tools and assets that could deliver it and any missing capability. Describe the smallest study or a simpler expressive route and say what observable result would support it. |

## Score, revise and compare

- In the working notes, score each applicable row of the rubric from 0 to 10 and give the specific observation or described cue behind it. Use **0** when there is no supporting cue, **5** when a cue only partly works or has a common unintended reading, and **8** when concrete cues work together at the current stage. For feasibility, **5** means a plausible route with an unresolved dependency or core behavior, and **8** means a credible route with its main risk and a bounded test identified. Mark checks that require playback or viewer feedback as untested. Mark requirements the work does not have as not applicable.
- Start from the weakness that most threatens the intended experience. Trace it to a choice in the draft. A faint detail may need more contrast. If the detail cannot carry the intended meaning at the viewing scale, consider changing the composition, material, action or relationship that carries it. Preserve creator requirements and revisit choices the draft introduced as needed.
- Make the revision and read the revised work across all four parts. Record the evidence that changed and rescore the subcriteria it affects, including regressions. Enlarging a familiar object may raise its visual impact while erasing the cues that tell us how it is used. If the fix creates a worse reading or simply shifts the original weakness elsewhere, rework the defining image and event together. Check the changed sense of timing, sound, interaction and cost as they apply. If another direction expresses the creator's intent more directly, try it and use the same rubric again.
- Continue the review and revision while a consequential weakness can be resolved on the page. Recommend the strongest direction with the change that earned the recommendation and the main remaining uncertainty. Keep feasibility separate and keep the evidence for the decisive subcriteria visible. Use the smallest visual, motion or sound study for what needs playback evidence. During preview, use [the experience checks](expression.md#review-the-experience). When the reading is uncertain, ask a viewer a neutral question about what they saw or heard before explaining the intent.
