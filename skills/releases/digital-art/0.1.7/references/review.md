# Four-part creative rubric

Use the same criteria to compare written directions and to improve the selected work. In a treatment, score the evidence described in words. In a preview, score what is visible, audible or felt in use at the intended viewing conditions.

## Precise expression

| Criterion | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Intent | Name what a viewer should notice, feel or reconsider. Start from what the creator has already decided. For a sparse brief, propose associations and a direction the creator can judge. |
| Image and association | Identify a familiar shape, material, action, cultural association or perceptible rule that gives the audience a way in. Say what the intended audience can infer from it. An abstract piece can establish a rule through density, resistance, repetition or another perceptible relationship. |
| Expressive mechanism | Point to the action or relationship that carries the idea. A screen pattern that becomes something a figure can pass through does several jobs within one event. Describe what the artwork would lose if the material, image or action were replaced. |
| Development | Name what changes in what the audience sees or understands. Look for a cause, a relationship between states, accumulating pressure, or a deliberate hold. In a still, read the progression through focus, scale and layers. Use exact times or values when they matter to the expression. A threshold, relative amount or response curve can guide the work when the experience determines the timing or magnitude. |
| Sensory coherence | Explain how the applicable choices in light, color, surface, motion, sound, text and interaction carry the idea together. If sound is present, identify a moment whose meaning or feeling it changes. If participation matters, identify what the gesture makes possible. |

## Visual appeal

| Criterion | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Composition and first glance | Describe one complete view, including its largest shapes, negative space, focal point and what the eye follows. At intended scale, check what is visible at a glance and what rewards a closer look. |
| Color and material | Name relationships between the colors and identify a property of the surface that is legible in light or motion. Assess the image in silence when it is also scored for sound. |
| Visual invention and recall | Point to the image, change in scale, juxtaposition or transformation worth pausing on. Identify the particular choice a viewer could remember after seeing the piece. |

## Emotional resonance

| Criterion | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Emotional entry | Identify a concrete moment or sensory detail that lets the viewer bring their own experience. Hair over a drain and water pooling around a foot offer a tangible reason for unease. |
| Emotional development | Describe how the feeling accumulates, holds or changes, and which moment produces that effect. For a still, compare the first impression with what becomes apparent on a longer look. |

## Production feasibility

| Criterion | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Feasibility of the defining effect | Name the hardest effect essential to the experience, the available tools and assets that could deliver it, any missing dependency, and a smaller study or simpler route that preserves the idea. Say what observable result would support the route. |

## Score, revise and compare

- Score each relevant criterion from 0 to 10 with a specific audience-facing observation or described cue. Use **0** when there is no supporting cue in the current draft, **5** when a cue exists but has a common unintended reading or only partly does its job, and **8** when concrete cues work together at the current stage. For feasibility, **5** means a plausible route with an unresolved dependency or core behavior, and **8** means a credible route with its main risk and a bounded test identified. Mark unavailable playback or missing viewer evidence as untested. Mark a criterion not applicable when the work genuinely has no corresponding requirement.
- Keep the four parts visible. Identify the strength to preserve and the weakness that most threatens the intended experience. Use the weakest consequential evidence to choose a concrete revision and a way to assess it. For example, recognizing thread, fabric and a tear recalls mending. Watching each stitch close one tear while opening another gives the work a way to express its cost. These are different things to review.
- Reassess the affected scores after the change and record what improved or remains uncertain. Compare alternatives on the intended experience, the strength of the defining image and the main risk. A feasibility score is a separate judgment. A high score elsewhere cannot compensate for a missing cue the work depends on.
- Before recommending a written treatment, make the changes that can be settled on the page. Reserve the smallest visual, motion or sound study for the chosen direction. During preview, use [the experience checks](expression.md#review-the-experience). When the reading is uncertain, ask a viewer a neutral question about what they saw or heard before giving them the intended interpretation.
