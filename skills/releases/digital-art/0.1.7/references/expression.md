# Precise expression

An evocative brief can leave interpretation open while being precise about what occurs in the piece. Locate the experience in an observable choice. What establishes the world? Which element carries the idea? What changes or stays deliberately still? What would the piece lose if that element were swapped for another?

- **An existing rule changes.** Learn the use or behavior the viewer already recognizes, then find the one change that gives it a second meaning. A material can retain the texture and arrangement of the original object while gaining a different behavior. Think through the point of overlap or contact at which the viewer recognizes both readings.
- **A reveal has a cause and a timing.** Decide which detail the viewer must recognize before a later one can change its meaning. A still can use occlusion, distance or the sequence of reading. A moving work can let one action cause the next. Repetition can steady, delay, aggravate or transform the same moment.
- **A material can carry the subject.** Try what survives erasing, how light grazes a surface, or what happens when layers meet or split. Let tests change the idea if the material suggests a clearer way to express it. Avoid taking the first plausible motif as the whole composition.
- **The viewer can complete the event.** Identify what the viewer’s gesture, movement, wait or refusal makes possible. Design feedback in the same visual language as the piece. If the work is already complete as a still or loop, leave room to look.

Treat finish as part of the expression. Decide what the eye follows in the full composition and where detail pays off. Flat color can produce a readable silhouette, directional variation can place light on a surface, and a local highlight can make one material legible. For Chinese and Latin typography, judge the actual scale, density, and weight together. If the words explain an action the image could reveal on its own, try putting that explanation into the artwork’s timing, composition or gesture.

## Use a recognizable image to give a complex feeling somewhere to land

Find what the intended audience could already recognize from a silhouette, an ordinary use, a material response or a culturally familiar image. With a sparse brief, propose several strong associations yourself. Read the creator's stated feeling and chosen objects as working constraints. Choose a clue that reads at viewing size and a visual relation or change that gives it significance here. Leave room for viewers' own emotional associations.

Hair knotted over a drain is readable from the tangle, its position over the opening and the water that fails to recede. The same distinct image may register as disgust, a minor task piling up, or feeling stuck. Such visual evidence gives each reading a place to begin. A depiction of a person crying supplies different evidence. Consider what the audience experiences that gives the sadness its particular weight.

Judge the picture on its own terms before recommending the idea. Describe its dominant shape, how light or color carries the eye, and a material or compositional detail that rewards a closer look. For a piece with sound, compare the image in silence and then explain what sound adds. When the idea relies on an extremely small change, find the perceptible surrounding action or composition that gives the detail meaning at the intended scale.

When a subject deliberately reads as two things, check both readings. At first glance, use the outline, proportion, light or another characteristic feature to establish the familiar object. At closer range, show the grain, marks or joints that establish how it is made. In motion, identify the moment the two readings meet. If one disappears when the framing or motion changes, restore the relevant visual clue without giving up the other. A concept frame may depict the finished object while the performance begins with the material it will be made from.

## Make the intent visible

Break the intention into the few relationships a viewer needs to perceive. A treatment can mix these choices:

- **A fixed constraint or cue.** Name what the artwork must align with, such as a recorded note onset, the edge of a physical screen, or a shape that must remain recognizable. Specify the required precision when it affects the experience.
- **A relationship or event.** Describe what causes the change, what stays linked and how the behavior changes. Use conditions, thresholds, relative sizes, asymmetry, or a shape of response such as quick at first and easing to rest. For a held gesture or a material response, the visible state can determine when the next event happens.
- **An outcome for review.** State what must read to the audience. Some outcomes follow from other choices: elapsed time from the motion, apparent weight from acceleration and overshoot, or apparent brightness from surrounding contrasts. Adjust the cause and assess the outcome at intended viewing size. Measure or specify a range when it helps compare alternatives.

A designer can control contact and flex in a folded surface while the resulting sense of weight is judged in playback. A print-like composition can preserve clear figure-ground separation at many viewports while the absolute size and brightness vary. Choose one or two changes to try when the intended reading fails. An internal control can guide iteration without becoming a public slider.

## Review the experience

Revisit the [creative rubric](review.md) using evidence from this preview. Update the affected scores and identify the change that would most improve the actual experience.

- State the intended experience in one sentence in the working notes. Preview the piece with explanatory copy hidden. Identify the visible or audible event from which a viewer might infer the theme. If there is nothing specific to recognize, revise the event or the composition before revising the copy. Keep text that does work of its own, such as a performed line, a meaningful label, or a necessary instruction.
- For motion or interaction, watch the opening and the change at normal speed in the intended viewer. Compare the actual states at viewing size. If the reveal needs a long wait, give the viewer a perceptible cue or reason to stay. Test the natural gesture and its response when the piece relies on one.
- When sound is part of the expression, decide what is heard at the point of contact and what develops across the scene. Useful foley follows the event that makes it happen. Music can connect actions, change pace or leave space for a turn to register. Enable sound with a normal click or tap and listen at normal volume. Check whether the sound reads at the point it is heard. A starting audio source or sample measurement verifies output. Listening establishes the perceptual result.
- Record any untested checks with the draft. If the environment cannot render motion or play audio, keep the draft open for review in an environment that can.

When trying directions, vary what the viewer encounters or how it develops. Keep the most coherent option and let small, focused tests settle refinements. For precedent, the local `references find` index includes links and notes on observable choices. Look at several mechanisms and keep screenshots or notes only as needed for the project.
