# Visual direction for data works

Read when the design language is open or the creator wants a broad change. Choose what the audience should first perceive, how close inspection changes the experience, and which parts of the piece must be read accurately. Build a coherent combination of choices that serves that experience.

| Choice | What to resolve |
| --- | --- |
| Composition and shape | Establish the visual center, silhouette, grouping, density, reading order, and relationships between full view and detail. Compare the overall structure at thumbnail size and the detail at actual size. |
| Color and light | Plan the value hierarchy, color roles, and where light falls. Flat fields, smooth transitions and view-dependent material color can coexist. Give each variation a purpose. |
| Marks and surfaces | Choose edges, overlap, line weight and texture based on what the image must preserve or reveal. Use specific surface behavior, such as layered pigment, ink bleed, reflected light or a crisp cut edge. |
| Typography | Establish what needs to be read and at what distance. Choose type, weight, spacing and alignment to support the work's voice. Tune Chinese, Latin and numerals by their visible proportions. |
| Space and movement | Choose flat drawing, layered depth, live space, stylized 3D rendered as 2D, or a combination. Decide what viewpoint, occlusion, movement and stillness do to the composition. |
| Sound and other senses | Consider whether ambience, silence, physical scale, touch or data-driven sound changes the experience. Decide which signals carry evidence and which shape the atmosphere. |

Use a reference to identify choices about specific parts of a work. A named artist, culture, genre or period can inform several dimensions. Locate the actual work, then describe its framing, shapes, material or rhythm in observable terms. Read the [creative decisions guide](creative-decisions.md) when comparing visual directions with the creator.

## Develop the frame before the page

Choose a subject or a relationship to stage. Plan where the eye lands, where it travels, and where it can rest. Try a second arrangement that changes the scale, crop, figure–ground relationship or point of view. A different palette on the same layout can settle a color decision. A different arrangement can test the character of the whole work.

Make at least one actual frame with data from the piece. At thumbnail size, inspect the silhouette, weight, negative space and point of attention. At full size, inspect how the image is built:

- Large structure establishes the gesture, shape, cluster, rhythm or spatial arrangement.
- Mid-scale structure gives the subject specificity. Use folds, bands, overlaps, a material transition, intervals, or distinct edges where they help the subject read.
- Fine detail rewards proximity. Use grain, fine marks, small highlights, wear or slight edge variation where the underlying form and the viewing distance support them. A spare work can succeed with very few such marks.

Decide what carries evidence at each scale. E.g., a long-view composition can preserve relative amounts through area, while a close view reveals individual observations with exact values. Surface finish, lighting and layout can be authored separately. Verify that their changes leave the intended comparisons readable.

## Inspect a real image and a changed state

Open a rendered frame before filling the surrounding page. Temporarily hide optional labels to assess the image on its own. Restore the labels needed to name the work, reveal a gesture or read the evidence. Choose type and spacing with the artwork visible, including Chinese, Latin and numerals when they appear together.

Look for a precise failure in the actual image. For example, thin details disappear at viewing size, contrast pulls the eye out of the frame, or a surface reads as smooth where it needs several layers. Change the cause and render the same view again. Compare at the same scale. For moving or interactive work, also inspect the meaningful changed state and try the action that produces it. Keep accepted composition, data and behavior through later revisions.

Choose the tools after the tests reveal what needs to be built. A single controlled viewpoint may allow a drawn or prerendered effect. A scene with changing light or perspective may need live geometry. A layered composition can combine static assets with a small live portion. Use the same frame checks whichever method you choose.
