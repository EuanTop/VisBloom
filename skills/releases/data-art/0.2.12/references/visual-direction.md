# Visual direction for data works

Use this reference for open compositions and substantial visual changes. When the creator has chosen a reference or supplied original work to reproduce, follow the relevant choices in that source.

## Choose the experience and its precision

A comparison needs stable perceptual cues where the viewer makes it. A work about a feeling can let timing, sound, spatial arrangement or a gesture carry meaning. The same piece can work at several levels. A distant view can make a relationship felt, a closer view can reveal how it was formed, and a detail can give the source value. Some pieces only need one of those views.

Find what gives the work a point of view. It could be a surprising data relationship, an ordinary gesture with a different consequence, or a particular image from the creator’s material. If changing the data leaves the main image or behavior essentially identical, check whether the data is playing the role the brief calls for. When the brief is open, try another plausible relationship or another way of giving it form before investing in the full production.

## Combine design choices

Consider only the dimensions that materially change this work.

| Dimension | What to decide |
| --- | --- |
| Color and light | Flat fields can make grouping or hierarchy clear. Gradients can carry a continuous change or describe illumination. Material color may vary with light, thickness or view. Decide which variations the viewer should read as data and which describe atmosphere or surface. |
| Mark and surface | Line weight, ink bleed, crayon grain, print texture and clean flat silhouettes all change what the eye picks out. Inspect what happens at edges, where marks overlap, and at the final viewing size. |
| Typography | Use weight, scale, spacing and serif or sans-serif contrast to establish the voice and reading order. In bilingual work, tune each script’s size and spacing to its visible proportions. Give captions and data labels the room their purpose requires. |
| Space and form | Choose among flat drawing, depth cues in 2D, live 3D, stylized 3D rendered to look drawn, and mixed 2D/3D. Use depth where viewpoint, occlusion, volume, light or scale is part of the experience. Plan which layers share a visual scale. |
| Cultural and historical references | Ground broad requests such as Ghibli-like, medieval, Chinese or Indian in specific works, periods, media or visual traditions. Identify choices such as framing, depth of painted backgrounds, manuscript margins, line rhythm or pigment relationships. Keep the cultural reference specific enough for the creator to recognize and discuss. |
| Encounter and emotion | Find what the viewer does or waits for, and what that action means. A pause, the disappearance of a mark, a message left for the next visitor or an unexpected response can carry the feeling. The piece may also need no input at all. |

Treat the dimensions as combinable choices. For example, a hand-drawn line and heavy serif can live with a sharp solid palette. A 3D object rendered with flat shading can sit among typeset notes and inked 2D marks. Where two directions are worth comparing, vary a consequential relationship, the visual hierarchy, the material behavior or the audience’s role. Match scale and source slice when that makes the difference easier to see.

## Revise from the actual work

Look at the opening view and a detail or meaningful change. Check where the eye lands, whether the reading order holds, whether the surface feels intentional and whether the audience can read what the work claims. For a piece built around a gesture, try the gesture and its response. For a static piece, use the final viewing or print scale. Record accepted design choices for later edits.
